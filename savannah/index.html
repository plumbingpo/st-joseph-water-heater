Getting More Years From Your Water Heater in Savannah

The best way to avoid a surprise leak is routine maintenance. We recommend that homeowners flush the tank once a year (more often with hard water), have the anode rod checked every three to five years, lift the T&P valve lever once a year to make sure it isn't stuck, and keep the thermostat around 120 degrees, which limits scalding risk and cuts down on scale. Tankless owners should plan on a descaling flush once a year if their water is hard.

Age matters too. Most tank water heaters last 8 to 12 years, tankless units often reach 20 with regular descaling, and heat pump models typically last 10 to 15. If your heater is past that window and showing symptoms such as rusty hot water, rumbling during heating, dampness under the tank, or hot water that runs out sooner than it used to, a planned replacement is almost always cheaper and much easier on you than an emergency one after the tank lets go.

If warning signs do show up, acting quickly keeps them small. A dripping relief valve might point to excess pressure or a missing expansion tank, both modest fixes. The same drip left alone for a season can keep stressing the tank until a seam gives way, and a small repair becomes a full replacement plus water damage. It's smart to keep our number saved on your phone so you can reach us at the first sign of trouble. Early intervention saves time, money, and cold showers.

Typical Water Heater Issues in Savannah

Water heaters in and around Savannah deal with a familiar set of problems. Depending on whether a home is on municipal water or a private well, hard water may be a real factor: calcium and magnesium settle to the bottom of a tank as sediment, insulating the water from the burner, which produces the rumbling and popping you may hear during a heating cycle. In tankless units, the same minerals build scale inside the heat exchanger, which is why regular descaling matters so much in hard-water areas.

We also see a lot of aging equipment. A tank-style water heater usually runs 8 to 12 years, and near the end of that window the anode rod has often been eaten away, leaving the steel open to rust. The area has a mix of fuel types, gas tanks in some houses and electric tanks in others, which means we service both sides: pilot assemblies, gas valves, and flues on gas units, and heating elements and thermostats on electric ones.

Code is another piece. Replacing an older heater frequently means updating the installation along the way: a thermal expansion tank if the water system is closed, a T&P discharge line that terminates correctly, a flue that drafts correctly on gas units, and a drain pan or seismic straps where required. Well water brings its own challenges, including heavier sediment, iron staining, and the sulfur odor that sometimes shows up when the water reacts with a standard anode rod. Every one of these problems can be fixed, and most cost far less when caught early. If something seems off, a quick diagnostic visit can keep a small problem from turning into a flooded floor.
